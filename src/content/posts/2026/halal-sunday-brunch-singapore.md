The room at Permata at Gedung Kuning is too beautiful to rush. The ceiling climbs more than three metres above the dining tables — nineteenth-century Palladian, warm with Sunday midday light, absorbing the sound of a full room before it becomes noise. At twelve-thirty, the first sitting is already full. Fifty dishes across five Nusantara stations, Chef Mel Dean's kitchen in the wings, ensuring that not one of them sits long enough to cool. A Javanese fish curry arrives before the satay. The laksa at the next station is different from the one you know.

This is what a serious halal Sunday brunch looks like in Singapore in 2026. Not a café with avocado toast and a MUIS stamp. Not a hotel buffet running the same satay section since 2014. A kitchen with something specific to say — about Nusantara cuisine, about wagyu provenance, about the logic of a French-Malayan tasting sequence — that has chosen Sunday to make its argument.

The shift happened gradually, then all at once. The case for halal brunch in Singapore used to end at a hotel buffet; over the past three years, the fine-dining halal scene — documented fully in [the 2026 halal fine-dining edit](/dining/halal-fine-dining-singapore-2026/) — extended its reach into the weekend lunch hour. Tasting menus that ran only at dinner now take reservations from midday. Wagyu specialists open at eleven. Omakase counters that run twelve-course sequences on Saturday evenings offer abbreviated, no-less-serious versions for Sunday lunch. The brunch hour, for halal diners in Singapore, is no longer a concession. It is a kitchen's second chance to make its case.

What follows is the Tatler-bar edit: ten rooms worth the Sunday reservation, organised by format. Every halal status declared plainly.

* * *

## What counts as a serious halal Sunday brunch in Singapore?

Three categories apply, and they are not interchangeable.

**Halal-certified (MUIS)** means Singapore's Islamic Religious Council has audited the kitchen — ingredients, sourcing, preparation, and cross-contamination protocols — across the full supply chain. This is the complete standard, and the only category that covers the kitchen end-to-end rather than just the menu description.

**Muslim-owned** means the restaurant was founded and is operated by Muslims, typically following Islamic dietary principles. MUIS certification is not always held — and some Muslim-owned kitchens serve alcohol on-site for non-Muslim guests. The category is broader than MUIS certification and narrower than "halal-friendly," which can mean almost anything.

**Pork-free or beef-free** is a partial accommodation. Relevant for Muslim diners, but not equivalent to either category above. Where this is the only applicable status, it is stated plainly — and Muslim diners should verify additional restrictions directly with the kitchen before booking.

The distinction matters most at brunch, when a kitchen is running a full room under time pressure and the sourcing decisions made at six in the morning determine what arrives at your table at noon.

## The Tasting Menu Brunch

### Restaurant Fiz

_Muslim-owned; pork-free; wine menu available for non-Muslim guests_ Tras Street, Tanjong Pagar | Lunch tasting menu from S$79++ | Saturday and Sunday service

The shortest route from midday to something worth the reservation in Singapore's halal dining scene is Chef Hafizzul Hashim's lunch menu. He runs the weekend format with the same discipline as the full Sajian Warisan dinner — condensed, not abbreviated. Bunga kantan appears as a reduction. Petai arrives as a note inside another dish's logic rather than a component in its own right. The sequence carries the structure of Malay shared dining inside a European tasting format: the flavour memory is Malaysian, the form is precise.

The room is narrow. Ten steps from table to kitchen. Sunlight hits the front window of the Tras Street shophouse at 1pm and holds for an hour. Allow ninety minutes. Order the full sequence.

_Halal note: Restaurant Fiz is Muslim-owned but is not MUIS halal-certified; alcohol is served on-site for non-Muslim guests. Diners requiring full MUIS certification should proceed to the entries below._

* * *

### The White Label Restaurant

_Halal-certified (MUIS)_ Telok Ayer Street | Approximately S$90–130++ per head | Weekend lunch service

Chef Hissyam Jaafar built The White Label as the most direct argument for French technique inside a MUIS-certified kitchen. The weekend lunch menu compresses that argument to its essentials. Galangal still runs through the starter. The Malayan flavour logic still organises the sequence from first course to last. The room is pale and linen-dressed. Service reads the table without intruding on it.

The White Label pioneered the halal trolley service in Singapore — a formal French gesture applied, without irony, to a fully MUIS-certified kitchen. That decision signals what kind of brunch this is. Reserve two weeks ahead for weekend tables.

_Halal note: MUIS halal-certified._

* * *

### Restaurant Espoir

_Halal-certified (MUIS)_ Duxton Hill | Approximately S$75–110++ per head | Weekend lunch

Street below, plate in front of you — the double framing of a Duxton Hill shophouse second floor is exactly what Espoir does at Sunday lunch. The kitchen blends classical European technique with Malayan ingredients: a cream-based starter that reveals local spice at its centre, a main course that references pandan through pastry logic rather than street food grammar. The value for MUIS-certified fine dining at lunch is strong. Twelve tables. Book by Thursday for the weekend.

_Halal note: MUIS halal-certified._

## The Wagyu Counter

Two kitchens in Singapore's halal dining scene are making the fine dining case for premium beef at weekend brunch. The argument differs in format and approach.

### Charr'd

_Halal-certified (MUIS)_ 324F Changi Road | Open from 11am Sunday | Approximately S$120–200+ per head

Singapore's first halal-certified A5 wagyu steakhouse imports whole Kuroge cows from Mie Prefecture — whole animals rather than portioned cuts, for freshness and full supply-chain traceability. Three cuts are available: ribeye, sirloin, tenderloin. Every sauce is halal-certified through the complete chain. At 11am on a Sunday, the room is quieter than it will be at seven in the evening. The investment is on the plate.

_Halal note: MUIS halal-certified; full supply-chain halal certification covers all imported wagyu._

* * *

### The Social Outcast

_Muslim-owned_ Central Singapore | Wagyu Experiential Omakase from S$125++ per person | Weekend by reservation

Nine cuts of A5 wagyu cooked over woodfire at the table, served piece by piece in the format logic of Japanese sushi omakase. The founding premise — a premium counter built around wagyu rather than seafood — produced something with no direct equivalent in Singapore's halal dining scene. Nine cuts. Nine conversations about grade, prefecture, and preferred internal temperature. At S$125++ per person, the most focused halal brunch investment on this list.

_Halal note: Muslim-owned; confirm current MUIS certification directly with the restaurant before booking._

## The Rooms Worth the Detour

Some Sunday decisions begin with the room and arrive, eventually, at the kitchen. Both of these earn their place on address and architecture alone — and then the cooking makes the case again on its own terms.

### Permata at Gedung Kuning

_Halal-certified (MUIS)_ 73 Sultan Gate, Kampong Glam | Chef Mel Dean | From S$58++ per head | Open Sunday lunch

The Yellow House — a nineteenth-century Palladian mansion beside the Istana Kampong Glam — is the most considered dining room in Singapore's halal circuit. The ceiling absorbs a full room's conversation. At noon, the light reaches the inner tables. Chef Mel Dean's kitchen is organised across five stations: more than fifty dishes drawing from the Indonesian, Malaysian, Bruneian, Thai, and Singaporean cooking traditions of the Malay archipelago.

The format is a buffet, which reads as casual until you examine the cooking standard at each station. Permata was a finalist for Best Halal Restaurant (Fine Dining) at the Epicurean Star Awards two consecutive years. At S$58++ per head for MUIS-certified fine dining in what may be Singapore's most beautiful dining room, the argument is complete.

_Halal note: MUIS halal-certified._

* * *

### The Malayan Council at Fullerton

_Halal-certified (MUIS)_ 3 Fullerton Road, Fullerton Waterboat House | Open from 11am | Modern Malay

Singapore River, Marina Bay, the Esplanade bridge — this room offers the most composed skyline the city can offer a dining table, and it places it directly in the frame from the moment you sit down. The Malayan Council's Fullerton outlet pairs it with Modern Malay and Singaporean heritage cuisine: Chilli Crab, Kueh Pie Tee, Seafood on Ice, dishes drawing from Singapore's full culinary range. This is the celebration table — a birthday brunch, a reunion Sunday, a meal that needs the room to carry part of its weight. The cooking is reliable. The location earns the rest.

_Halal note: MUIS halal-certified._

## The Levantine Table and the Omakase Counter

Two registers that have no equivalent in the casual halal brunch scene.

### Oud Restaurant

_Halal-certified (MUIS)_ Kandahar Street, Kampong Glam | Head Chefs Indra and Ridz | Approximately S$80–140++ per head

You smell the woodfire grill before the bread arrives. The open kitchen at Oud is positioned beside the dining room — not opposite it — and the cooking is part of the room's ambient experience from the first moment you sit. Head Chefs Indra and Ridz carry Michelin-kitchen experience into a format centred on fire, charcoal, and slow-cooked protein. The sourcing decisions on Kandahar Street are shaped by the district's history: the spice trade, the Arabic culinary tradition, the flavour logic of a port city's most cosmopolitan address. Built for a long Sunday lunch. The private dining format handles groups of six or more.

_Halal note: MUIS halal-certified._

* * *

### Ammakase

_Pork-free; beef-free — verify MUIS certification directly before booking_

#04-48, One Raffles Place | Saturday lunch and dinner, Monday–Friday lunch

The name combines _amma_ — mother in Tamil — with _omakase_: leave it to the chef. Chef Robin leads the world's first Indian omakase, Michelin-selected, drawing across the coastal cuisines of West Bengal, Gujarat, and the southern coast, plated through French technique. Six to ten courses. Nothing announced in advance. Saturday lunch is the access point.

The [direction of Singapore's halal fine dining](/dining/halal-fine-dining-singapore-2026/) — from Restaurant Fiz's Malay tasting sequence to Ammakase's coastal Indian counter — is not a trend. It is a kitchen culture that stopped building concession menus for Muslim diners and started building for ambition first, then declaring its halal status plainly.

_Halal note: Pork-free and beef-free throughout. Muslim diners must verify current MUIS certification and alcohol policy directly with the restaurant before booking._

## Practical notes: reservations and the Kampong Glam circuit

Reservations at every kitchen on this list are the rule, not the option. Restaurant Fiz and The White Label fill by Thursday for weekend tables. Permata at Gedung Kuning handles large groups well but requires advance notice for parties above eight. Charr'd opens at 11am without a fixed brunch format — early walk-in is possible, but a reservation by noon is recommended. The Social Outcast and Ammakase both require direct booking; Ammakase's halal status should be confirmed at the time of reservation.

For [modest travellers in Singapore](/style/modest-fashion-streetwear-southeast-asia-muslim-fashion-2025/) navigating a Sunday, the Kampong Glam corridor offers the most efficient MUIS-certified fine dining geography on the island: Permata at Gedung Kuning and Oud Restaurant on Kandahar Street sit within a ten-minute walk of each other, and Friday prayers at Sultan Mosque are three minutes from both tables.

The [quiet confidence that defines the best of restrained Asian luxury](/guides/the-complete-guide-to-quiet-luxury/) — craft over announcement, depth over texture — runs through the brunch service now the same way it runs through the Saturday evening. The format has changed. The ambition has not.

The [question of what this restraint actually signals](/style/why-quiet-luxury-is-fashion-biggest-contradiction/) in a market where louder is often easier applies to dining too: the most rigorous rooms are rarely the ones that announce themselves.

## The Editor's Pick

Permata at Gedung Kuning. Not because Chef Mel Dean's Nusantara spread is the most technically precise kitchen on this list — it is not. It earns the position because no other room in Singapore places a MUIS-certified kitchen, a building of genuine historical significance, and more than fifty dishes of Nusantara cooking in the same address at S$58++ per head. At that price, in that room, with that cooking standard, it is the clearest argument this city makes for halal fine dining on a Sunday.

The morning light through the high windows of the Yellow House at noon is the detail that stays. Arrive early. The room earns it.
