Before choosing a frame, choose a place. For a print or drawing you want to keep, the most useful starting point is a position away from direct sunlight, water risks and unstable conditions. The frame is the next decision; the arrangement comes after that.

For readers in Singapore and Southeast Asia, we compared Canadian Conservation Institute guidance with National Gallery Singapore's account of its collection care. The comparison suggests an order for making decisions at home. It does not establish a climate setting that will protect every artwork.

## Start with the wall

Light damage to paper cannot be reversed. The Canadian Conservation Institute advises keeping works away from sunlight entering through windows or skylights, using window coverings and considering UV-filtering glazing. It also explains that sustained high humidity can distort paper and encourage deterioration. Archival framing offers only short-term protection against increased humidity. [Read CCI's basic care guidance](https://www.canada.ca/en/conservation-institute/services/care-objects/paper-books/basic-care-art-paper.html).

CCI's [storage guidance](https://www.canada.ca/en/conservation-institute/services/conservation-preservation-publications/canadian-conservation-institute-notes/storing-works-paper.html) also identifies external walls, water pipes, heat sources and direct sun as locations to avoid. Our placement table translates those cautions into a household shortlist; the assessments are editorial judgements, not results from testing homes.

| If this is your available space | Our assessment | Your next decision |
|---|---|---|
| An interior wall away from windows and plumbing | A sensible place to start | Compare it with your other possible positions before arranging a group of pictures. |
| A wall crossed by direct sunlight | Choose another position | Do not treat a new frame as a reason to keep this location. |
| An outside wall or a position beside water pipes | Lower on the shortlist | Look for an interior alternative. |
| No suitable display position | Consider storage | Keeping a work need not mean displaying it continuously. |

This order also changes a styling decision. Rather than filling a conspicuous empty wall first, choose the least exposed location available and build the room around it. Our [console-table styling guide](/living/console-table-styling/) offers ideas for arranging objects once that location is settled.

## What the museum comparison tells us

In a [2020 National Gallery Singapore interview](https://www.nationalgallery.sg/sg/en/learn-about-art/magazine/ask-a-conservator-frequently-asked-questions-with-mar-cusso.html), paintings conservator Mar Cusso describes controlled collection environments, generally around 55% relative humidity, with a five-percentage-point tolerance, and 22°C with a two-degree tolerance. She stresses that different materials require different conditions. Paper and textiles are displayed at lower light levels, generally 30–50 lux, with display periods managed accordingly.

CCI's paper-storage note recommends below 50% relative humidity for general paper collections. The figures come from different institutional contexts. Neither page assesses your wall, frame or particular artwork. We would use them to understand the care involved, rather than lift one figure out as a guarantee for a home.

## Ask about the frame, then the box

A useful conversation with a framer covers the materials touching the artwork, how it is attached and how it is kept apart from the glazing. CCI's basic care page warns about poor-quality framing materials and adhesives; it describes archival matting and spacing between the image and glazing. Delicate charcoal and pastel need particular care. These are questions to discuss, rather than a reason to attempt unfamiliar mounting work yourself.

For works that are not displayed, CCI's storage note supports protective enclosures made from suitable acid-free materials and recognises that fragile or oversized pieces may need specialised housing. It advises consulting a paper conservator where necessary. Buying an attractive box is therefore a separate decision from establishing whether its materials suit the work.

The practical distinction is between arranging a room and caring for an object. A room can look finished while some works remain stored. Explore more considered approaches in [Arahkaii Living](/living/).
