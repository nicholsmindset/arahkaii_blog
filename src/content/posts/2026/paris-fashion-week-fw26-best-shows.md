Paris Fashion Week FW26 closed fashion month with sophomore collections — from Matthieu Blazy's caterpillar-to-butterfly Chanel to Chemena Kamali's folklore-drenched Chloé — that proved the debuts weren't flukes and rewrote a few rules along the way. Here's our ranking.

Paris Fashion Week is always the final word. After New York's commercial polish, London's creative chaos, and Milan's manufacturing excellence, Paris exists to remind everyone who runs the conversation. And FW26—running March 2–10 with 67 runway shows and 31 presentations—did exactly that, but with a twist: this wasn't a season of firsts. It was a season of seconds.

After 15+ creative director debuts defined SS26, FW26 was the sophomore test. Jonathan Anderson at Dior. Matthieu Blazy at Chanel. Michael Rider at Celine. McCollough and Hernandez at Loewe. The question wasn't whether these designers could generate excitement—they'd already proven that. The question was whether they could build something sustainable. Whether the vision had depth beyond the initial spectacle.

The answer, across almost every major house, was yes. And in some cases—Chanel, Dior, Chloé—the second collection was significantly better than the first. Here are the ten shows that earned their spot, ranked from great to greatest.

## 10\. Stella McCartney

[Stella McCartney](https://www.stellamccartney.com) staged her FW26 show inside a Parisian equine arena complete with a dozen black and white horses. The collection was '80s power suiting at full volume: peplums, stirrup trousers, sumptuous oversized coats, bedazzled denim, and iridescent fabrics. McCartney has always understood spectacle as storytelling, and the equestrian staging made a natural case for her brand's animal-free luxury positioning.

## 9\. Zimmermann

Nicky Zimmermann drew from her grandmother — a woman who worked as a lifesaver at Coogee Beach in 1920s Sydney at a time when women simply weren't lifesavers. That personal thread expanded into a broader tribute to trailblazing Australian women. The collection channelled that rebelliousness through the brand's signature bohemian maximalism: ruffled pants, lace-trimmed blouses, ballooning striped dresses, faux fur throws. The front row — Jessica Chastain, Naomi Watts, Gugu Mbatha-Raw — matched the collection's energy perfectly.

## 8\. Balmain (Antonin Tron Debut)

Antonin Tron's debut at Balmain was the season's most closely watched new chapter. The verdict: a confident, deliberate pivot away from predecessor Olivier Rousteing's maximalist flash. In their place: structured evening suits, moody embellished minis, lacy peekaboo detailing, and velvet jackets with cinched shoulders. Tron drew from the house's 1940s archives, filtered through Hollywood femmes fatales and what he called "minimal opulence."

## 7\. Loewe (McCollough & Hernandez)

Jack McCollough and Lazaro Hernandez left their own brand after 23 years to take over Loewe, and their sophomore collection confirmed the move wasn't a midlife crisis — it was an upgrade. Building on the colour-blocking and sleek leathers of their debut, FW26 deepened the house's tactile identity: leather craftsmanship met the intellectual, art-inflected wardrobe the designers built at Proenza. They're finding Loewe's new frequency, and it sounds increasingly like their own.

## 6\. Hermès

Nadège Vanhée's starting point was twilight: that threshold between day and night when colours deepen and the world feels suspended. At the Garde Républicaine headquarters, she recreated that atmosphere with a winding runway beneath a dusky sky surrounded by mossy greens. The palette mirrored the transition: sunset tones fading into crimson, then inky evening blues. In a fashion climate obsessed with shock value, Hermès chose atmosphere over spectacle — and proved that [quiet luxury](https://www.arahkaii.com/quiet-luxury-brands-worn-by-wealthy-insiders/) still has extraordinary depth when the craft is this good.

## 5\. Saint Laurent

Anthony Vaccarello set his show inside a modernist residence overlooking the Eiffel Tower. Bella Hadid led a cast embodying "nocturnal elegance" — louche tailoring, sharp tuxedos, and flashes of sheer lace anchored by Le Smoking. Vaccarello opened with bare-skinned tuxedo jackets before sending out further iterations punctuated by oversized crystal dove earrings. Sometimes the most powerful thing a fashion house can do is remind you why its icons became iconic in the first place.

## 4\. Celine (Michael Rider)

Michael Rider's approach to Celine continues to feel like the most emotionally intelligent creative direction in Paris. His FW26 show notes: "I love when messy, complex, layered inner lives come through underneath great clothes." That sensibility translated into slimmed-down silhouettes where the magic was in the styling. Rider is building a Celine that feels lived-in rather than aspirational — and that distinction is producing some of the most interesting fashion from any major house right now.

## 3\. Chloé (Chemena Kamali)

Chemena Kamali's FW26 collection for Chloé was a gorgeous collision of the house's signature carefree femininity with folklore and traditional costume references — silk chiffon, colourful tartan, ponchos, capes, and blouses that felt decisively Chloé while reaching somewhere deeper and wilder. Kamali has done something quietly brilliant: she's made the "Chloé girl" feel like a real person with a rich inner life, not a mood board. In a Paris season dominated by intellectual rigour and cinematic darkness, her warmth and whimsy felt like a radical counterpoint.

## 2\. Dior (Jonathan Anderson)

Jonathan Anderson staged Dior's FW26 show in the Jardin des Tuileries, constructing a circular runway around one of the park's ponds — populated with Monet-esque lily pads meticulously built to look real. The clothes felt like a natural evolution of his debut: bar jacket silhouettes and swooping folded skirts found their next iteration with florals, gingham, and polka dots. Anderson is doing something rare at Dior — honouring the house's couture DNA while making it feel playful rather than precious.

## 1\. Chanel (Matthieu Blazy)

Matthieu Blazy's sophomore collection for Chanel was titled La Conversation – Part Two, and it was exactly that: a deepening dialogue with Gabrielle Chanel that felt less like reverence and more like genuine creative friendship. His starting point was a quote from Coco in a 1950s Le Figaro interview: "Fashion is both caterpillar and butterfly. Be a caterpillar by day and a butterfly by night."

The collection opened with workwear-inspired overshirts and tweed blousons — caterpillar mode. Then the transformation: Jazz Age patchwork flapper dresses dusted with floral embroidery, furry coats in Sonia Delaunay-esque patterns, 3D floral embellishments that caught light on a holographic floor. Blazy yanked waistlines down to the hips, reimagining the Chanel suit as something looser, more intuitive, equally convincing on men and women.

Blazy has cracked what every Chanel creative director since Lagerfeld has struggled with: making the house feel culturally urgent without losing the codes that make it Chanel. The tweed is still there. The proportions are still there. But the woman wearing them? She's completely new.

## The Paris Fashion Week FW26 Verdict

Paris Fashion Week FW26 answered the question the entire industry has been asking since the great creative director reshuffle of 2025: was it worth it? The answer, emphatically, is yes. The sophomore collections — Blazy, Anderson, Kamali, Rider, McCollough & Hernandez — were almost universally stronger than the debuts. The new blood has settled in. The visions have depth. The houses feel alive.
