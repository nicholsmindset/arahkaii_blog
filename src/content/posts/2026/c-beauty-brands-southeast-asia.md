The narrative that dominated Singapore's beauty industry through the 2010s was simple enough to retell: Korean skincare conquered everything it touched. The ten-step routine, sheet masks as a category, glass skin as aspiration — K-beauty didn't just change what people bought; it redefined what beauty intelligence meant. That era isn't over. But something has shifted. Joocyee opened its first international flagship store on Orchard Road in July 2025. Florasis — known in China as Huaxizi — became the first Chinese cosmetics brand with physical space at both Samaritaine in Paris (September 2024) and Ginza Six in Tokyo (January 2025). Flower Knows secured a Proya investment in September 2025 and made its debut at Ulta Beauty in the United States. The infrastructure tells a story the retail data is starting to confirm. C-beauty's second wave isn't a K-beauty replacement — it's something more disruptive: an entirely different proposition built on craft, theatre, and cultural confidence rather than ingredient science and routine efficiency.

## What Made K-Beauty Dominant — and What It Left Behind

K-beauty's dominance was constructed on a specific and unusually coherent argument: skin science, ingredient transparency, and the democratisation of a professional-grade routine. From the time Sulwhasoo's fermented formulations first reached Singapore to the centella toner era of the early 2020s, the proposition was consistent — methodical, evidence-led, technically verifiable. You could follow the logic.

The ten-step routine, now a category shorthand more than an actual consumer habit, captured something real: that skin health was an investment, not an indulgence. It gave consumers a framework. And the K-beauty industry was disciplined about ingredient communication — niacinamide's whitening mechanism, tranexamic acid's function in addressing hyperpigmentation, propolis's barrier support. The beauty intelligence it demanded was chemical, not cultural. [The way K-beauty rewired the global beauty industry](/beauty/k-beauty-broke-the-algorithm/) from Seoul outward is one of the more complete market-transformation stories of the past two decades.

What K-beauty left behind was a specific emotional register. The relationship between K-beauty and cultural storytelling was always functional rather than theatrical. Ingredients were heroes. Routines were systems. Packaging was clean, legible, clinical — even Sulwhasoo's ginseng heritage was communicated through efficacy claims, not ceremony. What that created — and couldn't satisfy — was an appetite for beauty objects that operated on a different frequency. Not information, but feeling.

## Florasis and the Case for Luxury Theatre

Florasis was founded in Hangzhou in 2017. From the outside, it looks like a colour cosmetics brand. From the inside, it operates more like a cultural institution.

The brand uses traditional Chinese medicine ingredients — lotus seed oil, peony seed oil, pearl powder, jade powder — in formulations that are genuinely skin-compatible rather than decorative TCM references. But the ingredient story is almost beside the point. What Florasis built is theatre: each lipstick bullet is carved with motifs drawn from classical Chinese folklore; the cases reference traditional Chinese lock designs; the packaging employs wood carving masters, silversmiths, and ceramic artists. Lipsticks retail at approximately USD 50. Eyeshadow palettes at around USD 60. Neither price point is incidental — they position Florasis in the luxury tier, not the accessible-Asian-beauty tier.

The international expansion confirms the ambition. A counter at Samaritaine in Paris — an LVMH-owned property reopened in 2021 as a statement on French cultural retail — followed by a flagship at Ginza Six in Tokyo, making Florasis the first Chinese cosmetics brand with a physical address in both cities simultaneously. These are not wholesale-and-move decisions. Ginza Six is where Bottega Veneta's Tokyo concept operates. The neighbours were chosen deliberately. For those following [the argument that Asian craft heritage is a global luxury proposition](/style/korean-heritage-brands-renaissance/), Florasis is making the same case in beauty that third-wave Korean designers are making in fashion: that cultural specificity, done with rigour, competes on equal terms with European heritage.

## Why Flower Knows Captures Something K-Beauty Never Could

Where Florasis operates at a premium register, Flower Knows built its audience on emotional escapism — and in doing so, identified a gap K-beauty's science-led proposition couldn't fill.

Founded in Hangzhou in 2016 by cosplayers Yang Zifeng and Zhou Tiancheng, Flower Knows operates under the concept of 少女心 — loosely translated as "girlish heart," the emotional register of fairytale, wonder, and intentional softness. Products are named "Unicorn Tears" and "Cherry Love." Packaging invokes pastels and fantasy motifs. The brand's first two 2025 collections, Strawberry Cupid and Shell Pearl, both broke internal sales records; full-year 2025 revenue was projected to exceed 1 billion RMB (approximately USD 142 million). A September 2025 investment from Proya — one of China's largest listed cosmetics groups — provided the distribution infrastructure for international scale.

What's interesting about Flower Knows is what it isn't doing. It isn't claiming K-beauty's scientific authority. It isn't leaning heavily on guochao — the Chinese national chic aesthetic — as Florasis does. Its cultural reference is closer to a universal romanticised girlhood, which explains why it moved quickly through TikTok Southeast Asia before it had formal retail presence in the region. When assessing [the structural difference between C-beauty and K-beauty propositions](/beauty/c-beauty-vs-k-beauty-2026/), Flower Knows represents the dimension K-beauty rarely competed on: joy as a primary design objective. It is not making a claim about skin. It is making a claim about how a person feels when they open the case.

## Joocyee's Singapore Flagship and the Infrastructure Argument

The most concrete evidence of C-beauty's second wave isn't in search trend data or TikTok view counts. It is in real estate.

Joocyee — a JOY GROUP brand founded in 2019, positioning itself as "romantic premium" — opened its first international flagship store in Singapore in July 2025, following a softer entry in October 2024 through TikTok Shop, Shopee, and 31 Watsons locations across the city-state. The Singapore flagship is the brand's first physical address outside China. That is not a marketing activation; it is a distribution bet with real capital behind it.

The significance is structural. Joocyee didn't choose Singapore out of licensing convenience or regional partnership inertia. It chose Singapore because Singapore's beauty consumer is a reliable leading indicator for the wider region: high disposable income, high beauty sophistication, and a demonstrated track record of trialling non-European luxury before it reaches Bangkok, Jakarta, or Kuala Lumpur. For those following [Singapore's beauty retail through multiple cycles](/beauty) — J-beauty, K-beauty, indie Western — the physical flagship is a meaningful signal. A Joocyee performing well on Orchard Road tells a story about C-beauty's commercial viability that six months of TikTok Shop data cannot.

Alongside Flower Knows and Florasis, all three brands are now available at Watsons Singapore — a stocking decision that reflects established category demand rather than marketing investment. Watsons doesn't shelf speculative bets.

## The Proposition Difference: Why C-Beauty Isn't a More Affordable K-Beauty

C-beauty refers specifically to the generation of Chinese beauty brands founded post-2015 — of which Florasis, Flower Knows, and Joocyee are the most globally visible — built on the proposition that beauty is a cultural artifact, not just a cosmetic delivery mechanism. Comparing these brands to K-beauty as though one is a more accessible version of the other is the category error that persists in Western beauty commentary. The two propositions are structurally different.

K-beauty's authority is scientific and democratic. Its argument: everyone can have good skin with the right routine. The hero is the ingredient. The outcome is measurable — reduced hyperpigmentation, improved barrier function, addressed dullness. K-beauty's most persuasive communication has always been educational.

C-beauty's authority — at least in its most developed expressions — is cultural and theatrical. Its argument: beauty is an experience to be felt, not a problem to be solved. The hero is the object itself — the carved lipstick case, the fairytale palette, the "romantic premium" compact with its silk-textured surface. K-beauty gives you a routine. C-beauty gives you a ritual. In arahkaii's tracking of the category across Singapore's retail landscape, the [accessible tier of C-beauty](/beauty/beauty-best-chinese-makeup-brands/) — Judydoll, Flortte, and their equivalents — competes on price and trendability; the brands making the credibility argument compete on cultural weight. These are not the same market.

The evidence from Singapore's 2025 and early 2026 retail landscape suggests consumers aren't abandoning K-beauty's skincare logic — Sephora's 2026 partnership with Olive Young, rolling out dedicated K-beauty spaces across Singapore, Malaysia, and Hong Kong, confirms K-beauty is not conceding ground. What C-beauty is doing is different: adding a theatrical colour and ritual layer on top of K-beauty's established skincare base. The baskets are additive, not substitutive. For the [quiet luxury shift reshaping how Asian consumers assign value](/guides/the-complete-guide-to-quiet-luxury/), C-beauty's object-worth proposition fits a particular psychology: a willingness to pay for things that feel culturally anchored and intentionally made, even at price points that challenge fast-beauty assumptions.

## Where to Find C-Beauty in Singapore — and What That Tells You

The retail geography of C-beauty in Singapore currently breaks into three tiers, each telling a different story about where the category is going.

**Flagship and brand-owned:** Joocyee's standalone boutique is the only brand-owned C-beauty retail space currently operating in Singapore. The flagship environment is calibrated for discovery — the kind of considered space where spending SGD 80 on a lip product feels deliberate rather than impulsive. This is the format that builds brand equity, not just transactions.

**Mass-accessible:** Watsons stocks Joocyee, Flower Knows, Judydoll, and Flortte across its Singapore network. This is where most new consumers encounter C-beauty for the first time. Pricing spans the full category range — from Judydoll's accessible tier to the upper end of Joocyee's colour range, which competes at price points comparable to mid-tier Korean brands rather than drug-store equivalents.

**Online-first:** TikTok Shop remains the fastest channel for new C-beauty launches in the region. Florasis sells through its own website and regional e-commerce platforms. YesStyle carries the more niche brands like Girlcult and Cheeryep. The online channel built the Flower Knows Singapore audience entirely before any physical retail arrived. The logic of [investing in beauty objects with staying power](/guides/the-complete-guide-to-investment-dressing/) — rather than cycling through fast-beauty hauls — arrives in C-beauty's most intentional tier through exactly this channel.

What's notably absent: Sephora Singapore does not currently carry a dedicated C-beauty assortment. Given that Sephora's Olive Young partnership is specifically designed to deepen K-beauty's retail depth in Singapore, Hong Kong, and Malaysia, the C-beauty question at Sephora remains unanswered. When and how that relationship develops will be the definitive signal of whether C-beauty's second wave consolidates into mainstream retail infrastructure — or continues building from the challenger position.

The more revealing question isn't whether C-beauty displaces K-beauty in Southeast Asia. K-beauty's ingredient authority is too deeply embedded, and the Sephora-Olive Young partnership confirms the industry is doubling down rather than pivoting. The question is what C-beauty's theatrical proposition does to the wider definition of beauty intelligence. If beauty once meant knowing niacinamide from tranexamic acid, it now also means knowing why a lacquered compact case etched with Chinese folklore motifs is worth SGD 85. Florasis, Flower Knows, and Joocyee aren't winning a price war. They are expanding what beauty knows.
